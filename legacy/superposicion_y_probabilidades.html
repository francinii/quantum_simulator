Quantum Superposition: Amplitudes & Probabilities

A qubit can be a blend of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ at the same time. Move the state on the Bloch sphere, see how its amplitudes become probabilities, and then measure it many times to watch those probabilities emerge.

Bloch Sphere
Polar angle θ (controls the probabilities) 90°
Relative phase φ (does not change them) 0°

1. The State Vector

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle$$
|ψ⟩ = 0.707|0⟩ + 0.707|1⟩

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are amplitudes: they can be negative or even complex numbers. They are not probabilities yet.

Before measuring

Both possibilities coexist

The qubit is not secretly 0 or 1. It is described by two amplitudes at once, like an arrow pointing somewhere between the north and south poles of the sphere.

When measuring

The state collapses

A measurement always returns a single classical bit: 0 or 1. The arrow jumps to a pole, and the chance of each pole is the squared amplitude, $|\alpha|^2$ or $|\beta|^2$.

The hidden ingredient

Phase is invisible… for now

$|+\rangle$, $|-\rangle$ and $|+i\rangle$ all give 50/50, yet they are different states. The phase $\varphi$ only shows up when states interfere.

ψ Pedagogical Takeaway: Superposition is described by amplitudes; probabilities only appear when we measure, as their squared magnitudes.
